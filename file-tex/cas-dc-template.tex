\begin{abstract}
Accurate myocardial infarction (MI) localization from 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs) requires
models that can exploit both temporal morphology and the anatomical relationship between infarct
territories and ECG leads. This study revises a previous lead-attention ECG pipeline into ALPA-Net,
an Anatomical Lead Prior Attention Network for four-class MI localization: normal ECG class (NORM),
anterior-involved MI (AMI), inferior-involved MI (IMI), and lateral-involved MI (LMI). ECG records
from PTB-XL and PTB Diagnostic were harmonized into beat-level 12-lead inputs at 100 Hz. PTB-XL was
used for source-domain pretraining, and PTB Diagnostic was used for target-domain transfer learning
with five-fold cross-validation over the training-validation pool and a fixed held-out test set.
ALPA-Net combines a lead-wise shared CNN stem, multi-scale residual convolution, lead-token
construction, class-specific anatomical lead-prior attention, cross-lead and temporal Transformer
encoders, and attention pooling with a softmax classification head. The ALPA branch initializes
class-specific lead attention using clinically motivated lead priors; a no-attention variant is
included as an ablation. On PTB Diagnostic, full fine-tuning of ALPA-Net achieved a test macro-F1 of
$0.8596\pm0.0165$, balanced accuracy of $0.8588\pm0.0174$, and macro-AUC of $0.9682\pm0.0075$. The
best ALPA fold reached 0.8900 macro-F1. The no-attention partial fine-tuning ablation obtained the
highest mean macro-F1 ($0.8698\pm0.0208$), indicating that the present ALPA branch provides
physiologically interpretable attention but does not yet yield a consistent aggregate performance
gain. The results support anatomy-guided lead attention as a promising direction for interpretable
ECG transfer learning while identifying the need for stronger coupling between ALPA attention and
the classification objective. \textbf{GitHub Repository:}
\href{https://github.com/NugrohoRahmanto/myocardial-infarction-localization}{https://github.com/NugrohoRahmanto/myocardial-infarction-localization}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Myocardial infarction (MI) occurs when myocardial tissue is injured because coronary blood supply is
insufficient for the metabolic demand of the heart. In clinical practice, the electrocardiogram
(ECG) is one of the first tests used when MI is suspected because it is inexpensive, rapid,
non-invasive, and available in emergency and routine care settings. The diagnostic task is not
limited to identifying whether MI is present. Localizing the involved territory is also clinically
important because anterior, inferior, and lateral involvement reflect different spatial regions of
the myocardium, may suggest different culprit coronary distributions, and can guide subsequent
diagnostic attention.

The standard 12-lead ECG provides a spatial projection of cardiac electrical activity. Precordial
leads capture anterior and septal information, inferior limb leads provide evidence from the
inferior wall, and high-lateral or lateral precordial leads contribute information about
lateral-wall involvement. This spatial structure makes MI localization different from ordinary
multichannel signal classification. A model must learn temporal morphology, such as QRS and ST-T
changes, while also respecting the fact that not all leads carry equal evidence for every infarct
territory. Automated MI localization is therefore an anatomical evidence-localization problem in
which model decisions should remain sensitive to clinically meaningful lead groups.

Deep learning has improved ECG analysis by learning representations directly from raw or minimally
processed waveforms. PTB-XL provides a large annotated 12-lead ECG resource for supervised
pretraining and benchmarking
\cite{Wagner2020SciData_PTBXL,StrodthoffWagnerSchaeffterSamek2021JBHI_PTBXL_Benchmarks}, whereas the
PTB Diagnostic ECG Database remains a useful target-domain dataset for clinically oriented MI
evaluation \cite{Bousseljot1995PTB,Goldberger2000PhysioBank}. Nevertheless, transfer between these
datasets is non-trivial because acquisition conditions, annotation granularity, patient composition,
and class priors differ across cohorts. In the present prepared dataset, lateral-involved MI remains
difficult because its available records are heterogeneous and include lateral, antero-lateral,
infero-lateral, postero-lateral, and infero-postero-lateral patterns. This grouping is clinically
motivated because isolated lateral wall infarction is uncommon and frequently appears as part of
multiterritorial anterolateral, inferolateral, or posterolateral involvement
\cite{Ludhwani2024LateralWallMI}.

Recent ECG models have explored convolutional, recurrent, attention-based, and foundation-model
approaches
\cite{FengPiLiuSun2019ApplSci_MI_CNN_RNN,Hasbullah2023BioMedInformatics_MI_Hybrid_CNN_RNN,Cao2022FrontPhysiol_MSResNet_Attn_MI,HuBERTECG2024,McKeen2024ECGFM}.
These methods can be effective, but many treat the 12 leads as generic channels or rely on latent
attention maps that are not explicitly tied to clinical lead territories. This limitation is
especially relevant when class labels are heterogeneous. Lateral-wall involvement, for example, may
appear as isolated lateral MI or as part of antero-lateral, infero-lateral, postero-lateral, or
infero-postero-lateral patterns. A classifier that ignores this structure may obtain reasonable
aggregate performance while offering limited physiological transparency.

This motivates anatomy-guided attention: rather than allowing all lead weights to emerge without
prior structure, the model can be initialized with clinically informed lead priors for each
localization class. Such priors do not replace empirical learning; they provide an inductive bias
that reflects standard ECG localization knowledge and can make the resulting attention maps easier
to audit. This is valuable in biomedical machine learning, where a model's usefulness depends not
only on its score but also on whether its evidence pattern is plausible to clinical readers.

This study presents an ALPA-focused framework for MI localization. ALPA-Net denotes an Anatomical
Lead Prior Attention Network that computes class-specific lead attention using learnable class
queries and lead-prior logit bias. The current experiments use a softmax four-class formulation and
CrossEntropy loss. The evidence is interpreted conservatively: ALPA produces interpretable
class-specific attention diagnostics, but the no-attention ablation slightly outperforms ALPA on
aggregate mean macro-F1. The contribution of this paper is therefore a careful evaluation of
anatomy-guided lead attention under a controlled PTB-XL to PTB Diagnostic transfer-learning
protocol, not an unsupported claim that attention always improves performance.

The main contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We formulate ALPA-Net, a compact CNN--Transformer architecture with class-specific anatomical lead-prior attention for four-class MI localization.
  \item We harmonize PTB-XL and PTB Diagnostic into a beat-level 100 Hz representation with a four-class operational label space: NORM, AMI, IMI, and lateral-involved MI.
  \item We evaluate PTB-XL pretraining and PTB Diagnostic transfer learning using scratch training, frozen-backbone transfer, partial fine-tuning, and full fine-tuning.
  \item We compare ALPA-Net against a no-attention ablation, local CNN/RNN baselines, and external ECG foundation-model baselines using the saved experimental CSV outputs.
  \item We report macro-F1, weighted-F1, balanced accuracy, macro-AUC, class-wise F1, and attention diagnostics, and we explicitly discuss the limitation that the current ALPA branch is not yet optimized by an explicit attention-alignment loss.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work and Research Gap}
\subsection{Deep Learning for ECG and MI Analysis}
ECG interpretation has progressively shifted from handcrafted descriptors toward representation
learning from raw signals. PTB-XL has enabled large-scale supervised benchmarking and has
highlighted the influence of label design, diagnostic granularity, and class imbalance on ECG
performance
\cite{Wagner2020SciData_PTBXL,StrodthoffWagnerSchaeffterSamek2021JBHI_PTBXL_Benchmarks,PalczynskiSmigielLedzinskiBujnowski2022Sensors_FewShot_PTBXL,SmigielPalczynskiLedzinski2021Entropy_DL_PTBXL}.
MI-specific studies have used convolutional and recurrent networks for MI detection and localization
tasks
\cite{Gustafsson2022SciRep_MI_ED,Pham2023Sensors_Heartbeat_ArrMI,FengPiLiuSun2019ApplSci_MI_CNN_RNN,LuiChow2019IMU_MI_CNN_RNN_Portable,Hasbullah2023BioMedInformatics_MI_Hybrid_CNN_RNN}.
These studies motivate hybrid architectures because MI-related evidence can involve local waveform
morphology, cross-lead differences, and temporal variation.

However, MI localization has an additional anatomical structure that generic classification networks
may not exploit explicitly. Anterior involvement is expected to be reflected primarily in precordial
leads, inferior involvement in leads II, III, and aVF, and lateral involvement in leads I, aVL, V5,
and V6, with overlap patterns in antero-lateral or infero-lateral cases. This lead-territory
structure motivates ALPA: class-specific attention is initialized from anatomical prior
distributions rather than from an uninformed attention state. The goal is not to hard-code the
diagnosis, but to make the first attention state compatible with the diagnostic geometry of the
12-lead ECG and then allow data-driven fine-tuning to modify it.

\subsection{Attention, Recalibration, and ECG Transfer Learning}
Attention mechanisms are widely used to emphasize discriminative components of ECG signals.
Multi-scale ResNet with attention has been used for MI detection and localization
\cite{Cao2022FrontPhysiol_MSResNet_Attn_MI}, and attention-enhanced multi-scale feature optimization
has been explored for silent MI and atrial fibrillation detection
\cite{Cheng2024AIMLR_Attn_MultiScale_SilentMI_AF}. Other studies use multi-domain fusion and
channel-temporal attention to improve physiological-signal representation
\cite{Chen2025Biosensors_MDF_Fusion_MI,LimKim2025ApplSci_SpikeCAN}. SE-based recalibration has also
been used in ECG models to adaptively weight learned feature channels
\cite{Sun2024Sensors_CNNLSTMSE,QuFu2025JSMU_ResLSTM_TemporalSE}.

Transfer learning is important because labeled clinical ECG datasets are often small relative to
model capacity. Prior ECG transfer-learning studies show that reusable source-domain representations
can improve downstream classification, but only when preprocessing, label space, and fine-tuning
depth are aligned
\cite{WeimannConrad2021SciRep_TL_ECG,JangKimYoon2021HIR_TL_ECG,ChuiGuptaZhaoMalibariAryaAlhalabiRuiz2022Bioengineering_DistantTL_ECG,LopesBleijendaalRamosVerstraelenAminWildePintoDeMolMarquering2021CBM_TL_PLN,NguyenDo2025PLOSone_TL_ECG_Effective}.
Large ECG foundation models such as HuBERT-ECG and ECG-FM extend this idea
\cite{HuBERTECG2024,McKeen2024ECGFM}; however, their downstream utility depends on compatibility
between pretrained representations and the target task.

\subsection{Signal Processing Context}
Robust physiological classification also depends on preprocessing and representation stability.
Prior studies on ECG denoising, wavelet features, and broader physiological signal classification
show that preprocessing choices can materially affect downstream learning
\cite{MandalaFuadahArzakiPambudi2017ICoICT_ECG_Denoising,MandalaTresnasariLestari2022ICICyTA_ECG_Daubechies,MandalaWibowoAdiwijayaSuyantoZahidRizal2023ApplSci_DWBF_AF,MandalaCaiDiSunarAdiwijaya2020PLOSone_MVA_DecisionTree,MandalaCaiDi2017JMBE_MVA_Review}.
Related work on PPG, PCG, image, and sequential signal learning further supports the value of
selective representation learning and domain adaptation
\cite{ParsaoranMandalaPramudyo2022ICoDSA_PPG_Denoising,IhsanMandalaPramudyo2022ICoDSA_PPG_CHD,IqtidarQamarAzizKhan2021CBM_PCG_CAD,KommineniMandalaSunarChakravarthy2021JSC_FER_Hybrid,KommineniMandala2014ICACCI_CBirPreprocess,MandalaRamadhanIrsan2023COMNETSAT_SmartHome_CNN,Kim2023Sustainability_LSTMTL_Energy,Wang2020CSSP_LSTM_TL_RF}.
In this context, ALPA-Net is a task-specific attempt to align learned ECG representations with
clinically meaningful lead priors.

\subsection{Research Gap}
The literature leaves three gaps. First, many ECG attention models do not explicitly encode the
anatomical relationship between MI territory and ECG leads. Second, transfer learning studies often
report aggregate performance but provide limited analysis of attention behavior and class-wise
minority performance. Third, foundation-model scale does not guarantee downstream success when the
target label space and preprocessing differ. ALPA-Net addresses these gaps by introducing
class-specific anatomical lead-prior attention and by evaluating it against no-attention, local
baseline, and external pretrained baselines under the same generated dataset.

\section{Data Resources and Experimental Protocol}
\subsection{Source and Target ECG Datasets}
The source dataset is PTB-XL, and the target dataset is PTB Diagnostic. Both datasets are mapped to
four mutually exclusive operational classes: NORM, AMI, IMI, and LMI. In this manuscript, LMI is
used as the abbreviation for \emph{lateral-involved MI}, not only strict isolated lateral MI. The
broader label is used because lateral-wall evidence can occur alone or as part of antero-lateral,
infero-lateral, postero-lateral, or infero-postero-lateral infarction patterns. PTB-XL contains
11,722 prepared records and 132,028 extracted beats. PTB Diagnostic contains 273 prepared records
and 2,958 extracted beats. Table~\ref{tab:training_config} summarizes the prepared beat-level
distribution.

\subsection{Definition of the Lateral-Involved MI Label}
The lateral-involved label is defined transparently to avoid conflating a scarce strict
isolated-lateral subtype with a clinically broader lateral-wall involvement category.
Table~\ref{tab:lateral_involved_definition} lists the codes and sublabels included in this group. In
the current processed PTB-XL metadata, the final lateral-involved class contains 1,020 unique
records from 870 patients. This class is produced from PTB-XL diagnostic statements containing
lateral-wall MI codes: LMI, ALMI, ILMI, and IPLMI. The corresponding record counts are 201, 290,
478, and 51, respectively; patient counts are 185, 245, 416, and 50, respectively. Patient counts
are not additive because the same patient can contribute records with different lateral-related
statements. An archival data-preparation distribution further shows that the strict isolated
\texttt{lateral} clinical sublabel accounted for 105 PTB-XL records from 99 patients. Thus, the
previously assumed value of three PTB-XL records from one patient was not supported by the available
files. In the current PTB Diagnostic metadata, strict \texttt{lateral} appears in one record from
one patient, while \texttt{postero-lateral} appears in three records from one patient.

This grouping strategy reduces extreme sparsity while preserving clinically meaningful localization.
It is not intended to claim that all lateral-involved sublabels are identical. Rather, it defines
the fourth output class as the presence of lateral-wall involvement in any recognized
lateral-related pattern. The design also keeps the target label space compatible across PTB-XL and
PTB Diagnostic, where the available lateral-related statements differ in wording and frequency. This
choice is consistent with standard ECG localization knowledge, in which lateral involvement is
associated with leads I, aVL, V5, and V6, while overlap patterns may also include anterior,
inferior, or posterior lead territories
\cite{Ludhwani2024LateralWallMI,Thygesen2018FourthUniversalMI}. The limitation is that an error
within this class may mix several clinically distinct lateral-wall patterns; this is addressed in
the Discussion by interpreting LMI results as lateral-involvement performance rather than isolated
lateral MI performance.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Beat Extraction}
All ECGs are represented at 100 Hz. PTB Diagnostic records were downsampled from 1000 Hz to 100 Hz,
and final beat windows contain 65 samples and 12 leads. A Pan--Tompkins-style R-peak detector was
applied on Lead II, and each beat was cropped using a 0.20 s pre-peak and 0.45 s post-peak window.
Beat extraction increases the number of training instances, but it also creates correlated samples
because multiple beats can originate from the same recording. To reduce this risk, the split
protocol keeps beats from the same record within the same split. For PTB Diagnostic, the first and
last detected beats were removed to reduce boundary artifacts caused by incomplete windows or
unstable detector behavior near record boundaries.

Butterworth bandpass filtering was used for signal inspection and visualization. A fourth-order
0.5--40 Hz Butterworth filter suppresses baseline drift and high-frequency noise while preserving
dominant ECG morphology. Fig.~\ref{fig:butterworth_raw} and Fig.~\ref{fig:butterworth_response} show
the filtering example and frequency response.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
ALPA-Net is pretrained on PTB-XL and then fine-tuned on PTB Diagnostic. The fine-tuning notebook
combines the PTB Diagnostic training and validation records into a five-fold cross-validation pool
and evaluates each selected checkpoint on the same fixed held-out test split. Four transfer
strategies are evaluated: no pretraining, frozen-backbone transfer, partial fine-tuning, and full
fine-tuning. Model selection uses validation macro-F1 because the classes are imbalanced and because
minority-class errors are clinically relevant. Because all folds share the same held-out test set,
the reported fold mean reflects checkpoint variability across validation folds rather than
independent test partitions. The protocol is therefore best interpreted as a transfer-learning
stability analysis over five validation folds with a fixed target-domain test cohort.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics and Reporting}
The primary metric is macro-F1, which gives equal weight to NORM, AMI, IMI, and lateral-involved MI.
This choice is important because a model can obtain acceptable accuracy while still underperforming
on minority infarct territories. Weighted-F1 is reported to show performance under the empirical
class distribution, balanced accuracy summarizes class-normalized recall, and macro-AUC measures
ranking quality across the four one-vs-rest class comparisons. Class-wise F1 is reported for
interpretability because it reveals whether the aggregate score is driven by NORM or by genuine
infarct-territory performance.

The reported mean and standard deviation are computed across the five validation folds evaluated on
the same fixed test set. This design should not be interpreted as five independent test cohorts.
Rather, it quantifies how validation-fold selection and fine-tuning strategy influence the final
checkpoint when the target-domain test set is held constant. No statistical significance test is
reported; therefore, differences between models are described using cautious language such as
slightly higher, competitive, or more variable.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Overall ALPA-Net Architecture}
The final model is ALPA-Net: Anatomical Lead Prior Attention Network. It receives a beat-level ECG
tensor $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times L}$, where $T=65$ and $L=12$. The model uses a lead-wise
shared CNN stem, a multi-scale residual CNN block with kernels $3$, $7$, $15$, and $31$, a
lead-token builder, the ALPA module, a cross-lead Transformer encoder, a temporal Transformer
encoder, lead-time attention pooling, and a softmax classification head. The output classes are
NORM, AMI, IMI, and lateral-involved MI.

The architecture is designed around two complementary requirements. First, it must extract
beat-level morphology from each lead, because MI-related information can be expressed as localized
waveform changes. Second, it must preserve lead identity, because the same morphology has different
diagnostic meaning depending on the lead in which it appears. The shared CNN stem encourages
consistent morphology extraction across leads, whereas the lead-token and Transformer stages allow
the model to represent cross-lead relationships after lead-specific features have been built. ALPA
is inserted at the lead-token level so that anatomical prior information is applied to explicit lead
representations rather than to anonymous latent feature channels.

\subsection{Class-Specific Anatomical Lead Prior Attention}
Let $\mathbf{h}_l\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ denote the token for lead $l$. ALPA defines one learnable query
$\mathbf{q}_k$ for each class $k$. Class-specific attention scores are computed as
\begin{equation}
s_{k,l}=d^{-1/2}\mathbf{q}_k^{\top}\mathbf{h}_l + b_{k,l},
\label{eq:lwca_descriptor}
\end{equation}
where $b_{k,l}$ is a prior logit bias derived from an anatomical lead-prior matrix. The attention
distribution is
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{k,l}=\frac{\exp(s_{k,l})}{\sum_{j=1}^{L}\exp(s_{k,j})},
\label{eq:lwca_softmax}
\end{equation}
and the class-specific context is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{c}_k=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\alpha_{k,l}\mathbf{h}_l.
\label{eq:lwca_scale}
\end{equation}
The lead priors follow standard clinical territory assumptions derived from 12-lead ECG
localization: anterior involvement is primarily represented in the precordial leads V1--V4, inferior
involvement in leads II, III, and aVF, and lateral involvement in leads I, aVL, V5, and V6
\cite{Ludhwani2024LateralWallMI,Thygesen2018FourthUniversalMI}. These relationships are converted
into an initial attention-prior matrix over the lead order [I, aVL, II, III, aVF, aVR, V1, V2, V3,
V4, V5, V6]. NORM is initialized uniformly with $1/12$ mass per lead. AMI assigns 0.225 mass to each
of V1--V4 and small nonzero mass to the remaining leads. IMI assigns 0.30 mass to each of II, III,
and aVF and small nonzero mass to the remaining non-target leads. LMI uses a lateral-involved prior:
I, aVL, V5, and V6 each receive 0.1375 mass, auxiliary weights of 0.0667 are assigned to II, III,
aVF, V1, V2, and V3, V4 receives 0.05, and aVR receives zero. This design reflects the inclusion of
antero-lateral, infero-lateral, and infero-postero-lateral patterns while preserving the highest
weights on canonical lateral leads.

The prior matrix is used as a learnable logit-bias initialization rather than as a fixed constraint.
Consequently, ALPA provides a clinically motivated inductive bias and an interpretable starting
point for class-specific lead attention, but it does not guarantee improved predictive performance.
Final utility is evaluated empirically through the no-attention ablation and transfer-learning
results. Table~\ref{tab:alpa_prior_initialization} summarizes the implemented prior in a compact
form; the complete numeric matrix is stored with the manuscript figures as
\texttt{alpa\_initial\_attention\_prior\_matrix.csv}.

The current softmax implementation uses CrossEntropy loss for the four mutually exclusive classes.
The ALPA branch records class-specific attention and initializes the anatomical attention bias, but
no explicit KL-divergence attention-alignment loss is used in the final run. This implementation
detail is central to the interpretation of the results: the ALPA heatmap should be read as an
anatomical attention diagnostic, and the no-attention ablation is necessary to determine whether the
current branch improves predictive performance. Equations~\ref{eq:lwca_descriptor},
\ref{eq:lwca_softmax}, and~\ref{eq:lwca_scale} formalize the class-specific attention calculation,
and Fig.~\ref{fig:lwca_architecture} shows the resulting lead-attention diagnostic.

\subsection{CNN--Transformer Backbone and Softmax Head}
Each lead is first processed by a shared one-dimensional CNN stem. The resulting features are
projected to a common channel dimension and passed through a multi-scale residual CNN block. Lead
tokens are then constructed and passed to a cross-lead Transformer encoder. In parallel, pooled
temporal segments are processed by a temporal Transformer encoder. Lead and temporal representations
are aggregated by attention pooling and concatenated before the final softmax classification head.

\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{y}}=\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b}),
\label{eq:conv_encoder}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z}$ is the pooled lead-time representation. The training objective is CrossEntropy
loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{CE}=-\sum_{k=1}^{4} y_k\log(\hat{y}_k).
\label{eq:macro_f1}
\end{equation}
Equations~\ref{eq:conv_encoder} and~\ref{eq:macro_f1} define the final softmax output and the
corresponding training objective.

\subsection{Transfer-Learning Strategies}
Four adaptation strategies are evaluated. In no-pretrain training, all parameters are initialized
randomly. In frozen-backbone transfer, the pretrained backbone is loaded and frozen while the
classification head is trained. In partial fine-tuning, the early CNN feature extractor is frozen
and the higher-level attention, Transformer, pooling, and head components are trainable. In full
fine-tuning, all model parameters are updated. These strategies separate representation reuse,
adaptation depth, and overfitting risk.

\section{Discussion}
The results provide a nuanced view of anatomical lead-prior attention. ALPA-Net full fine-tuning
performs strongly and is competitive with the best no-attention ablation, but it is not the
top-performing configuration in the present run. This finding is scientifically useful because it
separates two questions that are often conflated in attention-based biomedical models: whether an
attention mechanism improves aggregate prediction, and whether it provides a clinically
interpretable evidence pathway. In the current implementation, ALPA is valuable for the second
question and competitive for the first, but it does not yet establish a consistent performance
advantage.

This suggests that the current ALPA design is useful as an interpretable anatomical attention
branch, yet its attention signal is not sufficiently coupled to the classification objective to
consistently improve macro-F1. This interpretation is supported by the implementation: ALPA computes
class-specific lead attention and class-context vectors, but the final softmax classifier is
optimized only by CrossEntropy loss on the pooled lead-time representation.

The strongest ALPA result is full fine-tuning, not frozen transfer. This indicates that the
pretrained PTB-XL representation must be adapted substantially for PTB Diagnostic. Frozen-backbone
transfer is limited because only the classification head adapts, whereas the target data differ in
label distribution, beat extraction, and lateral-involved definitions. Partial fine-tuning shows
high variability, suggesting that freezing the early CNN while updating higher modules may sometimes
preserve useful morphology but can also restrict the model when the target domain differs strongly
from PTB-XL.

The no-attention ablation is important for scientific interpretation. It outperforms ALPA by
approximately 0.0102 macro-F1 in the best mean comparison (0.8698 versus 0.8596), which is small but
non-negligible. No statistical significance test was performed, so this difference should be
described as an observed aggregate difference rather than a confirmed superiority claim. Therefore,
the manuscript should not claim that ALPA improves aggregate classification performance in its
current form. Instead, the appropriate conclusion is that ALPA provides a physiologically structured
attention mechanism and competitive performance, while the current evidence motivates future work on
stronger integration, such as adding an explicit attention-prior loss, feeding class-context vectors
into the classifier, or using territory-aware auxiliary supervision.

The class-wise results are also informative. ALPA full fine-tuning achieves high LMI F1, but
lateral-involved performance must be interpreted cautiously because the PTB Diagnostic lateral test
records are limited and beat-level samples are not independent at the patient level. The
lateral-involved class also aggregates several anatomical patterns. This improves feasibility for
learning but reduces label granularity, so a correct prediction only indicates lateral-wall
involvement, not necessarily correct discrimination between isolated lateral, antero-lateral,
infero-lateral, and postero-lateral variants. NORM, AMI, and IMI performance is more stable across
models, whereas LMI is more sensitive to split design and lateral-involved label definitions. This
class-wise behavior reinforces the need to report macro-F1 and balanced accuracy rather than
accuracy alone.

Several limitations remain. First, the final dataset is beat-level and record-wise; beats from the
same record are kept within one split, but beat-level samples are correlated. Second, the fixed PTB
Diagnostic test set is small, especially for strict lateral-only records. Third, ALPA attention is
initialized from anatomical priors but is not currently supervised by a dedicated
attention-alignment loss. Fourth, the lateral-involved label is clinically motivated but still
coarse; it cannot replace finer clinical annotation when the research question requires separating
lateral-only from multiterritorial involvement. Fifth, the current external foundation-model
comparison depends on local preprocessing and wrapper implementation; it should not be interpreted
as a universal benchmark of HuBERT-ECG or ECG-FM.

These limitations point to concrete next steps. ALPA should be evaluated on larger external cohorts
with patient-level splitting and independent test sets. The attention branch should be coupled more
directly to prediction, for example through explicit prior-alignment loss, class-context
classification, or territory-aware auxiliary objectives. The lateral-involved label should be
revisited when more records are available, allowing a hierarchical formulation that first detects
MI, then identifies territory involvement, and finally separates overlap patterns. Model calibration
and uncertainty estimation should also be examined because a clinically useful localization system
should communicate when evidence is weak or conflicting. Finally, ALPA heatmaps should be reviewed
with cardiology expertise to determine whether the learned lead emphasis is clinically plausible
beyond numerical performance.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presents an ALPA-focused study for MI localization from 12-lead ECGs. The clinical
motivation is that MI localization is inherently spatial: anterior, inferior, and lateral
involvement are expressed through different lead groups, and a useful model should account for this
structure rather than treat all leads as interchangeable channels. ALPA-Net addresses this problem
by introducing class-specific anatomical lead-prior attention into a compact CNN--Transformer
architecture and by evaluating PTB-XL pretraining followed by PTB Diagnostic transfer learning.

The strongest ALPA configuration is full fine-tuning, achieving $0.8596\pm0.0165$ test macro-F1,
$0.8588\pm0.0174$ balanced accuracy, and $0.9682\pm0.0075$ macro-AUC. The no-attention partial
fine-tuning ablation slightly outperforms ALPA with $0.8698\pm0.0208$ macro-F1, indicating that the
current ALPA branch provides interpretability and anatomical structure but has not yet produced a
consistent aggregate performance advantage. This mixed result is central to the contribution of the
paper: clinically informed attention can make model behavior more transparent, but its predictive
value depends on how strongly the attention pathway is integrated with the training objective.

Future research should validate ALPA-Net on larger and more diverse ECG cohorts, use stricter
patient-level external evaluation, and refine the lateral-involved label when more granular clinical
annotation is available. Methodologically, adaptive clinical priors, explicit attention-alignment
objectives, territory-aware auxiliary heads, uncertainty estimation, and cardiologist review of
attention maps are natural extensions. These directions directly follow from the present findings:
ALPA provides a plausible anatomical inductive bias, but stronger coupling between prior-guided
attention and class prediction is needed before anatomy-guided attention can be claimed to improve
MI localization performance consistently.
